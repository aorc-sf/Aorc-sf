GENEALOGY & DESCENDANT RESOURCE CENTER

Start documenting your family line.

This resource center is designed to help descendants begin organizing family-history evidence before formal state certification systems are fully operational. AORC does not certify eligibility and does not make official descendant determinations.

AORC Family Research Starter Path

Begin with what you know and work backward one documented generation at a time.

1 • Start with yourselfRecord your full name, birth information, parents, and known places.
2 • Build generationsAdd grandparents, great-grandparents, and earlier generations with dates and locations.
3 • Find recordsUse census, vital, military, church, probate, deed, newspaper, and archival records.
4 • Connect evidenceDocument how each generation connects to the next. Track conflicting records instead of hiding them.
5 • Preserve the fileKeep copies, citations, source links, research notes, and unresolved questions together.

Records to search

Federal census records, Freedmen's Bureau records, Freedman's Bank records, U.S. Colored Troops files, birth and death certificates, marriage records, probate and wills, deeds, church records, newspapers, slave schedules, plantation records, and county archives.

Use family stories as leads; use records as evidence.

Research log

For every search, record the ancestor, repository, collection, search terms, date searched, result, citation, and next step. A good log prevents repeated work and makes research reviewable.

Document negative searches too.

Evidence chain

Build a lineage chain that shows Person A → evidence → Person B → evidence → Person C. Each claimed parent-child connection should have supporting documentation whenever possible.

A family tree is a map; evidence is the proof.
What should I collect first?
  • Names, nicknames, maiden names, and spelling variations.
  • Approximate birth, marriage, and death dates.
  • Cities, counties, states, churches, schools, military service, and occupations.
  • Birth/death certificates, obituaries, funeral programs, family Bibles, photographs, letters, and old addresses.
  • Names of older relatives who may know family history.
Why is the 1870 Census important?

For many African American families, the 1870 federal census is the first post-emancipation census in which formerly enslaved people appear by name in general population schedules. It can be an important bridge to earlier records, but research methods vary by family and location.

What if names, ages, or birthplaces conflict?

Do not force the records to agree. Record each version, evaluate who created the record and when, seek independent corroboration, and write a short explanation of why one conclusion is better supported than another.

Can AORC tell me whether I officially qualify?

No. AORC can provide public education, research navigation, and preliminary genealogy assistance. Official eligibility or descendant certification must be determined by the legally authorized government program using its final rules and procedures.

Should I send DNA data to AORC?

No. AORC is not collecting DNA or genetic-test data at this stage. Family-history research can begin with documentary records while official standards and privacy procedures continue to develop.

Privacy guardrail: Petition/supporter information is not the same thing as genealogy client information. AORC's future genealogy work should use a separate, access-controlled system with written consent, retention rules, and clear limits on what personal documents are collected.

FLAGSHIP POLICY FRAMEWORK

The American Ownership & Reparative Capital Plan

The plan begins with a specific obligation: repairing the economic injury created by American chattel slavery and government-sanctioned exclusion that followed it. It is designed around ownership rather than permanent dependency.

Restitution through ownership

Housing, investment capital, business capitalization, education, trust capital, recurring benefits, and documented family-specific restitution.

Permanent productive capital

Invest part of the capitalization in an independently governed permanent trust rather than spending every dollar once.

Build more housing

Pair reparative purchasing power with construction, rehabilitation, community ownership, and vacancy activation.

Create more businesses

Turn reparative capital into employers, suppliers, commercial property, tax revenue, and productive enterprise.

Fiscal discipline

Use progressive financing, institutional restitution, limited borrowing, verified savings, investment returns, and sunset mechanisms.

Economic autonomy

The goal is household power: enough capital to make meaningful choices, survive shocks, invest, build, and pass assets forward.

Policy-model guardrail: Dollar figures, participation assumptions, benefit values, investment returns, housing estimates, and financing allocations presented by AORC are working policy scenarios unless separately sourced as external facts. They are not enacted benefits, legal entitlements, guaranteed returns, or official government scores.
PUBLIC EDUCATION

Frequently asked questions

What is AORC?

A founding-stage public-interest initiative being developed as a hub for genealogy resources, public education, research, community organizing, reparative economic-development strategy, and implementation support.

Who is the descendant-based framework intended for?

Principally descendants of persons enslaved in the United States, using evidence, administrative standards, and due process. Final eligibility rules for any government program must come from the authorized public agency.

Is this just a cash-payment plan?

No. Direct cash can have a role, but AORC's broader strategy emphasizes capital formation through homes, businesses, investments, education, land, productive assets, and permanent trust capital.

Does AORC currently provide legal or official eligibility determinations?

No. Information on this site is educational. AORC is not a government agency and does not currently issue official genealogy certifications, legal opinions, or benefit determinations.

Why emphasize documentation before programs fully launch?

Family-history research can take time. Starting carefully now can help descendants preserve records, identify missing links, and become better prepared for future official processes without claiming that current research guarantees eligibility.
